Conscious Realism, the Conscious Agent, and the Ontological Status of Assertion

A TACE analysis of Hoffman's claim that spacetime is not fundamental, the identity of the subject that “says” this, and the distinction between linguistic production and conscious intentional assertion.

1. The starting point: spacetime is not fundamental

One of the central claims of Donald Hoffman's Conscious Realism is that spacetime is not fundamental. Hoffman describes the assumption that spacetime is fundamental as a major assumption in science and states that Conscious Realism rejects it. He also points to developments associated with quantum gravity and “positive geometries” as evidence that mathematical structures may lie outside ordinary spacetime and even beyond conventional quantum theory. This assertion is fully compatible with the current ontology of TACE. TACE does not regard spacetime as fundamental, not even within the Physical Realm. Created reality comprises two complementary and inextricably interlocked domains: the Realm of Forms and the Physical Realm. Its current hierarchy places Universal Formal Geometry, belonging to the Realm of Forms, prior to topology, spacetime, and physical fields, which characterize the Physical Realm. Q-Forms constitute the principle of potentiality and instantiation, and topology first appears through their organization. Thus, the transition from Pure Geometry in the Realm of Forms to Topology in the Physical Realm marks the distinction between two lawful modes of participation: Formally Transparent Participation and Topologically Organized Participation.

Thus, TACE does not merely assert that space-time might be emergent—not ex nihilo, but constitutionally. Its current ontology already assigns space-time a derivative status. Space-time belongs to the historically realized physical order rather than constituting the ultimate ontological foundation of reality. Consequently, TACE aligns with the basic thrust of Hoffman’s claim while situating it within a broader ontological hierarchy.

2. The semantic problem: who “says” that spacetime is not fundamental?

Hoffman's formulation is significant because the grammatical subject of the assertion is not “Hoffman” but Conscious Realism:
“Conscious realism is saying that’s not true.”
At first sight, this appears to make a non-human entity the subject of speech. But this does not, by itself, establish that Conscious Realism is a conscious entity. The construction is ordinary metalinguistic shorthand. Philosophers and scientists routinely say that a theory “says”, “claims”, “holds”, “predicts”, or “entails” a proposition. The theory is a representation of an ontology and a system of propositions. It should not automatically be treated as an ontological subject.

3. The deeper issue: Conscious Realism's actual ontological subject

The issue becomes substantially more interesting when one moves from the phrase “Conscious Realism says...” to the ontology of Conscious Realism itself. Conscious Realism takes conscious agents as fundamental. A conscious agent is formally characterized through processes of perception, decision, and action. These are represented by Markov kernels connecting world states, experiences, and actions.

Ontological question: The kernels P,D,A mathematically describe perception, decision, and action—but do the kernels themselves perceive, decide, and act? If yes, what is a kernel ontologically constituted of?
The fundamental entity in this formalism is therefore not necessarily a human being. It is a conscious agent. Human beings can be conscious agents, but the category is not intrinsically restricted to humanity. This gives the original semantic observation a more precise form. The theory itself is not necessarily the speaker, but the ontology of Conscious Realism identifies conscious agents as fundamental subjects. In TACE, a subject is not identified merely by the existence of formal transitions or kernels. It is a constitutionally bounded unity: the tetrahedral organization whose vertices are Appetite to Observe, Reflexivity, Scientia, and Intentionality, with G-Consciousness expressing the resulting constitutional unity.


It is highly interesting to determine whether all the entities that Hoffman's Conscious Realism treats as conscious agents—including the hypothetical entities associated with DMT—meet the requirements set forth by the TACE Subject tetrahedron. Moreover, it is important to determine how we might establish whether such entities are capable of experience at all. This gives us a genuinely interesting research program: Conscious Realism provides the candidate class; the TACE Subject provides the constitutional diagnostic; and controlled experimentation provides the means of validation. But first, the following question must be answered:
Can controlled investigation establish, independently of the investigator's own experience and theoretical assumptions, that a candidate conscious agent is capable of experience and satisfies the constitutional conditions of the TACE Subject?

4. Theory, proposition, author, and ontological subject must be separated

At least four levels should be distinguished: 
1. Proposition
   “Spacetime is not fundamental.”

2. Theoretical framework
   Conscious Realism

3. Human author / advocate
   Hoffman and other proponents

4. Ontological subject
   A conscious agent, according to the Conscious Realist ontology
These levels must not be collapsed. The theoretical framework can be said to “say” something in a metalinguistic sense without thereby becoming a conscious agent. The ontological claim that conscious agents are fundamental is a different proposition.

5. Why the machine case is different

Suppose a machine produces the sentence: 
“Spacetime is not fundamental.”
The machine has undeniably produced a linguistic token. But the production of the token does not, by itself, establish that the machine is conscious. TACE therefore needs to distinguish several levels: 
Linguistic production ≠ Semantic representation ≠ Rational judgment≠ Conscious intentional assertion
A machine may generate a sentence through computation, reproduce a proposition, or perform formal inference without there being sufficient evidence that the machine possesses phenomenal consciousness. The correct formulation is therefore not:
“Machines can speak even when they are incapable of consciousness.”
That would prematurely settle the question of machine consciousness. The more defensible formulation is:
Machines can produce linguistic utterances even when their possession of consciousness has not been established.

What makes a formally specified perception–decision–action system an ontological subject of experience rather than merely a system that can be mathematically described in those terms?
This does not refute Conscious Realism. It identifies the precise point at which its mathematical formalism and its ontological interpretation must be distinguished.

12. Final formulation

The strongest version of the argument can therefore be stated as follows:
Conscious Realism does not merely claim that consciousness is fundamental; it formalizes its fundamental entity—the conscious agent—as a perception–decision–action system represented by Markovian kernels P, D, A. Consequently, the relevant ontological subject is not necessarily human but a conscious agent. However, the mathematical existence of a P-D-A architecture does not by itself demonstrate consciousness. It establishes a formal agent structure; the additional claim that such a structure is genuinely conscious is an ontological claim of Conscious Realism.
Accordingly, when Hoffman says that “Conscious Realism” says that spacetime is not fundamental, the theory itself should not be treated as a conscious speaker. The phrase is metalinguistic. But the ontology underlying the theory does force the deeper question of which conscious agent is the actual subject of experience, judgment, and assertion. That question becomes especially significant when the assertion is generated by a machine. The machine's production of the proposition is not sufficient to establish that the machine is a conscious subject. The relevant issue is whether the machine possesses the ontological conditions necessary for conscious intentional participation. For TACE, therefore, the decisive philosophical question is not simply:
“Is spacetime fundamental?”
but also:
“What constitutes the subject capable of knowing, judging, and intentionally asserting that spacetime is not fundamental?”
This is where the TACE distinctions among Intelligence, Consciousness, etc. and lawful historical participation become relevant. TACE can accept the non-fundamentality of spacetime while refusing to identify formal computation, linguistic production, or Markovian dynamics with conscious subjectivity.
